The Conspectus Of Facts

2. The petitioner, a retired officer of the Armed Forces, rendered more
than thirty-four years of continuous and unblemished service and was released
from service on attaining the age of superannuation on 01.01.1997. At the
time of retirement, the petitioner was suffering from Stage-I Hypertension,
however, during the Release Medical Examination conducted on 18.09.1996
he was erroneously placed in medical category SHAPE-1 and no disability
was recorded. Subsequently, upon review of medical records and
consultations with specialists in the year 2018, it came to light that the
condition had progressed to Stage-II Hypertension with ischemic attacks and
that the disability had escaped proper assessment at the time of release. The
petitioner thereafter asserted that he had become entitled to consideration for
disability pension under the applicable pensionary rules and the Guide to
Medical Officers (Military Pensions), 2008.

3. The petitioner pursued his claim before the authorities, which was
rejected, compelling him to approach the Armed Forces Tribunal by filing
Original Application No. 1134 of 2023 seeking grant of disability pension.
The Tribunal dismissed the application solely on the ground of delay by
relying upon Union of India v. Ex. Sep. R. Munusamy, Civil Appeal No.
6536 of 2021, reported as 2022 INSC 731 without entering into the merits of

the claim.

Analysis

15. The present writ petition is founded essentially on a plea of parity
with the judgment rendered by this Court in Smt. Joginder Kaur v. Union of
India (Supra). The petitioner contends that since relief was granted in the
aforesaid case, the same benefit ought to be extended to him. The submission,
though attractively presented, cannot be accepted when tested on settled
principles governing finality of litigation, delay in service claims, and the
statutory scheme governing disability pension.

16. At the threshold, the present proceedings are clearly barred by the
principle of res judicata. The petitioner had earlier challenged the dismissal of

his claim before the Armed Forces Tribunal by filing CWP No. 29179 of 2024

before this Court. The said writ petition was dismissed. A review petition
preferred thereafter was also dismissed. The petitioner further approached the
Supreme Court by way of a Special Leave Petition, which too came to be
dismissed. Consequently, the issue relating to the petitioner’s claim has
already been examined by competent courts at successive stages and has
attained finality. The present petition, which seeks to reopen the very same

controversy on the same cause of action, is therefore clearly not maintainable.

17. The principle that repeated writ petitions on the same cause of
action are impermissible is too well settled to require elaboration. The doctrine
of res judicata, though codified under Section 11 of the Code of Civil
Procedure, applies with equal force to proceedings under Article 226 of the
Constitution in order to prevent multiplicity of litigation and abuse of the
process of the Court. In the present case, the petitioner having unsuccessfully
pursued his remedies before this Court and thereafter before the Supreme
Court, cannot be permitted to reagitate the same issue by initiating fresh

proceedings.

18. In T.P. Moideen Koya v. Government of Kerala 2004(8) SCC 106,
the Supreme Court held that the doctrine of res judicata applies with full force
even to proceedings under Article 226, so as to prevent multiplicity of
litigation and abuse of the process of court. Relevant extract is here as under:

It is well settled that a decision pronounced by a Court of competent
jurisdiction is binding between the parties unless it is modified or
reversed by adopting a procedure prescribed by law. It is in the
interest of public at large that finality should attach to the binding
decisions pronounced by a Court of competent jurisdiction and it is

also in the public interest that individuals should not be vexed twice

19. Equally untenable is the parity alleged by the petitioner upon the
judgment rendered in Smt. Joginder Kaur (supra). In the said case, the
husband of the petitioner had been invalided out of military service by the
medical board on account of schizophrenia, the disability being assessed at
40%, which constituted the very basis of the claim raised before the Court.
The factual foundation of the present petition, however, stands on an entirely
different footing. In the instant case, the petitioner had already retired from
service and was not discharged on account of disability, and therefore the
disability was not the cause of cessation of service. The essential circumstance
which formed the basis of adjudication in Joginder Kaur namely, invalidation
from service due to disability, is thus conspicuously absent in the present case.
It is well settled principle that Article 14 of the Constitution does not envisage
mechanical extension of relief granted in another case where the factual
matrix is materially different. In Chandigarh Administration v. Jagjit Singh
1995 SCC (1) 745, the Supreme Court held that an order passed in favour of
one individual cannot be relied upon to claim similar relief when the factual

circumstances differ. Relevant extract is here as under:

Of course, if in case the order in favour of the other person is found
to be a lawful and justified one it can be followed and a similar
relief can be given to the petitioner if it is found that the petitioners'
case is similar to the other person's case. But then why examine
another person's case in his absence rather than examining the case
of the petitioner who is present before the court and seeking the
relief. Is it not more appropriate and convenient to examine the
entitlement of the petitioner before the court to the relief asked for
in the facts and circumstances of his case then to enquire into the

situations - would neither be advisable nor desirable. In other
words, the High Court cannot ignore the law and the well-accepted
norms governing the writ jurisdiction and say that because in one
case a particular order has been passed or a particular action has
been taken, the same must be repeated irrespective of the fact
whether such an order or action is contrary to law or otherwise.
Each case must be decided on its own merits, factual and legal, in

20. There is yet another formidable obstacle confronting the petitioner.
The claim suffers from gross and unexplained delay of nearly twenty-three
years. The petitioner retired in the year 1996. The claim for disability pension
was raised decades thereafter. Courts exercising writ jurisdiction have
consistently declined to entertain stale claims in service matters.

21. The statutory framework governing disability pension also militates
against the petitioner’s claim. Regulation 54 of the Pension Regulations for

the Army 1961 is relevant, and it is as under:-

the competent authority, be granted in addition to his/her retiring
pension/gratuity a disability element at the rate appropriate to the
accepted degree of disablement and the rank last held with effect from
such date as may be decided upon in the circumstances of the case.

22. This regulation provides that disability pension becomes admissible
only when a disability is attributable to or aggravated by military service and

is assessed by a duly constituted medical board.

23. A bare perusal of Regulation 54/86 will reveal that such provision
has been incorporated in the Pension Regulations for the Army 1961 so that
the persons who were discharged in a fit medical condition from the Armed
Forces Services, and if they are found to be suffering from any disease
subsequent to their discharge from service, and the disease is having
connection with the Military Service and acceptable as attributable to Military
Service, such Army personnel may also get the benefit of the disability
pension. The obvious reason is that the law framers must have conceived the
situation that, a person from the Army, Air Force or Navy, may be discharged
in a fit medical condition, SHAPE-1, but subsequently, it may be noticed that
such a person suffered the disease, during the service period and his disease

had direct connection with his service conditions. Such disease can be noticed

24. Important fact is that the person's disability must have surfaced or
have been noticed subsequent to discharge but must have been attributable to
or aggravated by the Military Service, and it may surface during the service,
or it may surface after service, but it should surface within a period of 7

In the case at hand, the Release Medical Examination conducted prior to
retirement placed the petitioner in medical category SHAPE-1 and recorded
no such disability. The statutory requirement having been fulfilled at the time
of retirement, the presumption of correctness attached to the medical

assessment cannot be lightly displaced decades later.

25. The petitioner seeks constitution of a Re-Survey Medical Board for
reassessment of disability at this distant point of time. Such a course would be
wholly impracticable and contrary to the underlying scheme of the pension
regulations. Medical conditions, as is well known, evolve with age. A
reassessment conducted decades after retirement would inevitably reflect
deterioration attributable to the natural process of ageing rather than
conditions existing at the time of discharge. The rationale is evident: an
exercise undertaken after several decades cannot reliably assess if the medical
disability is indeed attributable to military service. Reliance may be placed on
the judgement of the Apex court in Union of India v. Talwinder Singh 2012
