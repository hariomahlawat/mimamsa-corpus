1, Prayer
The present petition is filed under Articles 226/227 of the

Constitution of India seeking issuance of a writ of certiorari for quashing the
termination order dated 19.05.2017 (Annexure P-2) and the appellate order

benefits including continuity of service and back wages.

4, Analysis
The Court, upon consideration of the pleadings and rival

contentions, finds that although the impugned order dated
19.05.2017(Annexure P-2) is styled as a termination simpliciter under Rule
5(1) of the CCS (Temporary Service) Rules, 1965, a close scrutiny of the
factual matrix clearly reveals that the termination is founded on alleged
misconduct attributed to the petitioner. The record unmistakably shows that

the immediate cause for termination was the petitioner’s short absence from

leave was not sanctioned, coupled with the respondents’ perception that the
petitioner was not interested in serving the force.

It is well settled that when an order of termination is founded on
allegations of misconduct, indiscipline or unsuitability, the veil can be lifted to
ascertain its true nature. In the present case, the respondents themselves have
justified the termination by alleging repeated attempts by the petitioner to seek
discharge, misleading conduct, and unauthorized absence, all of which clearly
amount to imputations affecting the petitioner’s character and suitability for
service in a disciplined force. Such termination, therefore, cannot be treated as
termination simpliciter and necessarily attracts the requirement of adherence
to principles of natural justice.

The Court further finds merit in the contention of the petitioner that
no show-cause notice was issued nor any departmental inquiry conducted
prior to passing the impugned order. Even a temporary government servant is
entitled to protection against arbitrary and punitive action when the
termination is founded on misconduct. The invocation of Rule 5(1) of the CCS
Rules cannot be used as a camouflage to dispense with an employee on
allegations which are punitive in nature, without affording him an opportunity
of hearing.

Moreover, in the facts of the present case, reliance placed by the
respondents on the CCS Rules does not hold good in law. The petitioner was
an enrolled member of a disciplined armed force governed primarily by the
CRPF Act and Rules. When a specific statutory framework under the CRPF
Rules exists to regulate service conditions, resort to the CCS Rules to justify
termination on allegations of misconduct is impermissible. The impugned
action, therefore, suffers from legal infirmity, as the CCS Rules could not

Further, the action of the respondents is unsustainable in view of
Rule 16(1)(a) of the CRPF Rules, which governs the period of service and
discharge of members of the Force. Under the said provision, during the initial
period of engagement, a member may be discharged on one month’s notice by
the appointing authority, but such power cannot be exercised arbitrarily or as a
substitute for imposing punishment for alleged misconduct. Once the
respondents themselves attribute indiscipline and lack of interest in service to
the petitioner, the discharge ceases to be a simple administrative action under
Rule 16(1)(a) and assumes a punitive character, thereby mandating

compliance with principles of natural justice.

The absence attributed to the petitioner was for a very short duration
of two to three days and occurred in the backdrop of his marriage, which is a
significant personal event. The respondents have failed to demonstrate that
such brief absence, by itself, warranted the extreme penalty of termination,
particularly when the petitioner had already rendered more than two years of
service/training. Even assuming the absence to be unauthorized, such a minor
lapse could, at best, have warranted a minor penalty. The extreme action of

termination, particularly after the petitioner had undergone substantial training
